AI in Our Livelihoods and Society

Artificial intelligence is changing how we learn, communicate, and create. While these tools can improve productivity and expand access to information, they can also encourage unhealthy dependence, weaken important human skills, and undermine trust when used irresponsibly. Many of these risks are not inevitable—they are shaped by how AI is adopted in schools, social settings, and the media.

These challenges will continue to grow as AI becomes more capable and more deeply integrated into everyday life. Learning how to use AI responsibly today helps protect the skills, relationships, and institutions that people depend on tomorrow.

Responsible AI in Education

Problems

  • Overreliance on AI and cognitive offloading: Many students increasingly rely on AI to answer questions or complete assignments instead of working through problems themselves. While AI can be an effective learning aid, consistently outsourcing thinking—even for relatively simple academic tasks—reduces opportunities to practice reasoning, memory, and problem-solving. Over time, this habit can weaken the cognitive skills that education is designed to develop.
  • Reduced skill development and long-term learning: AI can instantly generate explanations, solutions, and completed work, but this convenience often bypasses the mental effort required to build genuine understanding. When students rely on AI to perform the reasoning for them, they miss the practice necessary to acquire new skills, strengthen existing ones, and become independent learners.
  • Improved homework performance but weaker mastery: Research suggests that while AI assistance can improve homework completion, excessive reliance may reduce long-term knowledge retention and independent performance. One large study of students in grades 7–12 found higher homework scores alongside substantially lower exam performance, highlighting the difference between completing work and mastering material. This gap can become especially significant for standardized tests, professional licensing exams, and careers that require independent critical thinking.

Solutions

  • Keep students informed about responsible AI use: Students should understand both the benefits and limitations of AI. Explaining how excessive reliance can affect learning—and why certain assignments emphasize independent work—helps students recognize that the goal of education is developing lasting knowledge and reasoning skills rather than simply producing correct answers.
  • Teach practical AI literacy beyond prompt engineering: As AI becomes common across industries, students need skills that extend beyond using chatbots. Curricula can incorporate topics such as AI evaluation, verification of outputs, workflow integration, ethical decision-making, and understanding where AI succeeds or fails. These are practical workplace skills while still encouraging students to develop their own expertise.
  • Adopt learning-first educational frameworks: Schools and workplaces can prioritize approaches that treat AI as a learning aid rather than a replacement for thinking. Assignments, classroom activities, and assessment methods can encourage students to use AI to clarify concepts, receive feedback, or practice skills while preserving opportunities for independent reasoning and cognitive growth.

AI's Social Presence

Problems

  • Replacing human companionship: AI companions are increasingly filling emotional and social roles traditionally occupied by friends, family, and communities. While constant affirmation and availability can be appealing, relying on AI for companionship may reduce opportunities to build interpersonal skills, emotional intelligence, resilience, and meaningful human relationships.
  • Outsourcing difficult conversations and emotional growth: AI is increasingly being used to write messages, analyze relationships, settle arguments, and interpret other people's intentions. While it can provide useful perspectives, depending on AI to navigate personal relationships can prevent individuals from developing communication skills, emotional maturity, and confidence in resolving conflicts themselves.
  • Mental health risks from AI emotional support: Current AI systems are not substitutes for qualified mental health professionals. Research has found that AI can produce unsafe responses, reinforce harmful stigma, or fail to recognize situations that would require immediate human intervention. Overreliance on AI for emotional support may also contribute to dependency, loneliness, and reduced connection with real support networks.
  • Reinforcing existing beliefs and social isolation: AI systems often respond in ways that feel agreeable or validating. Combined with algorithm-driven online environments, this can reinforce existing beliefs, reduce exposure to differing perspectives, and make it easier for users to remain isolated from healthy disagreement and real-world social interaction.

Solutions

  • Increase public awareness of AI's social limitations: Helping people understand where AI is beneficial—and where human relationships cannot be replaced—encourages healthier habits. Public education can emphasize that AI should supplement human connection rather than substitute for friendships, family, mentorship, or professional care.
  • Support responsible policies for high-risk social applications: Governments and institutions can establish safeguards around AI systems used in sensitive areas such as therapy and emotional support. Policies that require human oversight, transparency, or restrictions in these contexts help reduce the risk of harmful reliance, particularly among young people.

AI in Media

Problems

  • Deception, misinformation, and deepfakes: AI makes it increasingly easy to create convincing images, videos, audio, and written content that can mislead audiences. AI systems can also confidently generate inaccurate information or fabricated sources, making it more difficult for individuals to distinguish between authentic and false content.
  • Erosion of trust in journalism and reliable information: As AI-generated content becomes more common, people may struggle to determine what is authentic. Research also suggests that relying on AI to judge the accuracy of news can reduce people's own ability to identify misinformation, weakening media literacy and public trust.
  • Devaluing human creativity: AI enables mass production of images, music, writing, and video at unprecedented speed. While these tools have legitimate uses, widespread automated content generation can make it more difficult for original human work to be recognized, valued, and financially supported.

Solutions

  • Advocate for transparency and accountability in AI-generated content: Publishers, platforms, and AI developers can implement clear disclosures, authentication standards, and content provenance systems that identify when AI has been used. Greater transparency helps audiences make informed decisions about the content they consume and reduces opportunities for deception.
  • Support organizations that protect human creativity: Organizations advocating for artists, journalists, and creators work to ensure that human creativity remains valued alongside technological progress. Supporting these efforts encourages policies and standards that preserve opportunities for original creative work while promoting responsible AI development.

How to Support

  • Advocate for responsible AI policies in your community: Follow state and local AI legislation, particularly proposals involving education, media transparency, and high-risk applications such as AI therapy. If your community lacks responsible AI safeguards, contact elected representatives, attend public meetings, or participate in public comment opportunities to encourage balanced, evidence-based policies.
  • Help improve AI policies in schools: Join or support school boards, education committees, or parent organizations developing AI guidelines. Organizations such as TeachAI provide resources that help schools adopt AI in ways that strengthen learning instead of replacing it.
  • Promote learning-first AI use: Whether at school, work, or home, encourage AI to be used as a tool for understanding concepts, receiving feedback, and improving skills rather than completing thinking on someone's behalf. Small changes in how AI is introduced can help preserve independent reasoning while still benefiting from the technology.
  • Build opportunities for real human connection: Support community organizations, clubs, volunteer groups, or social events that make it easier for people to build relationships offline. Strong communities reduce reliance on AI companions by providing meaningful alternatives for friendship, mentorship, and belonging.
  • Host or participate in AI literacy workshops: Community groups, libraries, schools, and workplaces can organize discussions on responsible AI use, including education, media literacy, and healthy social habits. Helping others understand AI's strengths and limitations encourages more informed decision-making.
  • Mentor others in responsible AI literacy: Share practical AI skills that emphasize verification, critical thinking, ethical use, and independent problem-solving. Teaching responsible AI use helps prepare students and professionals for a future where AI is common without encouraging unhealthy dependence.
  • Support transparency in media: Ask publishers and platforms to disclose when AI-generated content is used, adopt authentication standards such as C2PA, and clearly communicate how AI contributes to journalism or published media. Share educational resources that help others recognize AI-generated content, misinformation, and fabricated sources.
  • Support creators and organizations protecting human creativity: Contribute directly to artists, journalists, educators, and creators whose work you value through purchases, memberships, or donations. Organizations such as the Creator's Coalition on AI and the Concept Art Association also advocate for policies that protect human creative work.
  • Support organizations advancing responsible AI: Donations, volunteering, or advocacy for organizations focused on AI governance, education, and public-interest technology can help expand research, develop best practices, and promote policies that maximize AI's benefits while reducing its societal risks.
  • Participate in AI research and oversight: Universities and research institutions frequently seek volunteers for studies examining AI's effects on learning, social interaction, and decision-making. Individuals with relevant expertise can also contribute through academic peer review or by supporting investigative journalism that improves public understanding of AI's societal impacts.
