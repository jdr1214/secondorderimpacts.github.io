Second Order Impacts About

The infrastructure, operations, and supply chain of AI hardware puts the technology on track to cause our planet irreversible harm.
But it's not too late.

Environmental Harms - and what you can do

AI does not exist only in the digital world. Every AI model depends on physical infrastructure: data centers filled with computing hardware, power plants supplying electricity, cooling systems consuming energy and water, and mines extracting the materials needed to manufacture chips, servers, batteries, and other equipment.

As AI use expands, so does the environmental footprint of this infrastructure. Data centers place additional demand on electrical grids, cooling systems can consume enormous amounts of water, and producing new hardware requires mining and processing minerals that can damage ecosystems and surrounding communities.

These demands are projected to grow. But the environmental impact of AI is not inevitable. Cleaner electricity, better cooling technology, responsible hardware reuse, and stronger standards for AI infrastructure can reduce these harms.

The public also has a role. Individuals can participate in decisions about where and how data centers are built, support technologies that reduce their environmental footprint, recycle electronics responsibly, and support organizations working on more sustainable infrastructure.

Here is where the problems are—and where action can make a difference.

Problems

  • Climate and air pollution — AI data centers require enormous amounts of electricity, and that electricity can come from fossil fuels. Data centers contributed about 1% of global energy-related greenhouse gas emissions in 2025, according to the London School of Economics' Grantham Research Institute. AI inference—the process that occurs when people actually use deployed AI systems—accounts for an estimated 80–90% of AI computing resources. That means AI's environmental footprint does not end once a model has been trained: everyday use continues to require electricity. When that electricity comes from grids dependent on coal, oil, or natural gas, burning those fuels releases greenhouse gases and other air pollutants. As AI usage and data center construction increase, additional electricity demand could therefore translate into additional emissions unless data centers transition toward lower-carbon sources of power.
  • Water demand — cooling data centers can put significant pressure on local water supplies. Computers generate heat while operating, and thousands of high-performance processors running together generate an enormous amount of it. Data centers therefore require industrial-scale cooling systems. Some facilities use evaporative cooling, in which water absorbs heat as it evaporates. While effective, this process can consume substantial quantities of water. Global AI training and use have been projected to account for approximately 4.2–6.6 billion cubic meters of water withdrawal in 2027. Because data centers are concentrated in particular locations, the consequences are also local: facilities can compete with residents, agriculture, businesses, and ecosystems for limited water resources. Alternatives exist, but their environmental tradeoffs differ. Air-based cooling, for example, can reduce direct water consumption while requiring additional electricity.
  • Mineral extraction and hardware — building AI infrastructure creates environmental damage long before a server is switched on. AI infrastructure requires semiconductors, servers, storage systems, batteries, electrical equipment, and other hardware containing materials such as silicon, boron, lithium, gallium, graphite, and numerous other minerals and metals. Extracting and processing these resources can require large amounts of energy and water. Depending on the material, location, and mining practices involved, extraction can contribute to greenhouse gas emissions, groundwater depletion, water and soil contamination, deforestation, erosion, habitat destruction, and land degradation. Replacing hardware also creates electronic waste. When usable equipment and valuable materials are discarded instead of repaired, reused, refurbished, or recycled, more resources must ultimately be extracted to manufacture replacements.

Solutions

  • Transition data centers to cleaner electricity. Reducing AI's climate impact requires reducing the fossil-fuel emissions associated with the electricity that powers it. Expanding access to existing low-carbon energy—including renewable sources such as wind and solar—and connecting data centers to cleaner grids can reduce emissions from their operation. The goal is not simply to produce enough electricity for growing AI demand, but to meet that demand without locking communities into additional fossil-fuel generation.
  • Support the development and deployment of nuclear energy where appropriate. Nuclear power can provide large quantities of low-carbon electricity and could contribute to supplying the continuous electricity demanded by data centers. Continued research, development, deployment, and appropriate regulation of nuclear technologies can expand the range of low-carbon alternatives available to grids facing rapidly increasing electricity demand. Nuclear development has its own costs, risks, waste-management requirements, and implementation timelines, so it should be evaluated alongside other low-carbon energy sources rather than treated as a single solution to AI's electricity footprint.
  • Replace water-intensive cooling with liquid cooling where appropriate. Liquid cooling transfers heat away from computing hardware using fluid rather than relying primarily on cooling the surrounding air. Direct-to-chip cooling circulates liquid through cold plates attached directly to processors and other high-heat components. Immersion cooling places hardware in electrically nonconductive dielectric fluid that absorbs heat directly. These approaches can reduce reliance on water-intensive evaporative cooling and can improve cooling efficiency, particularly for increasingly powerful AI hardware.
  • Use dry cooling to reduce direct water consumption. Dry cooling replaces evaporative systems with air-cooled heat exchangers, allowing facilities to reject heat without continuously consuming water through evaporation. This can substantially reduce operational water demand, making it particularly relevant in water-stressed regions. The tradeoff is that dry cooling can require more energy or become less efficient under certain climatic conditions, meaning facilities should consider both their water supply and the environmental impact of their electricity.
  • Use hybrid cooling systems when neither liquid nor dry cooling alone is sufficient. Hybrid systems combine cooling technologies to reduce dependence on water while maintaining the temperatures required by high-density computing equipment. Liquid can remove heat directly from processors, while air-based systems handle remaining heat. Data centers can therefore select cooling methods based on local climate, water availability, computing density, and electricity sources rather than treating large-scale water consumption as an unavoidable cost of operating AI infrastructure.
  • Reuse, refurbish, and recycle electronic hardware. Extending the useful life of servers and other electronics reduces both electronic waste and demand for newly mined materials. When equipment can no longer be reused, responsible e-waste recycling can recover valuable materials and keep hazardous components out of landfills. IT Asset Disposition (ITAD) programs can also refurbish usable equipment, resell components, donate technology, and properly recycle hardware that has reached the end of its useful life.
  • Invest in material-substitution research. Researchers can investigate alternatives to materials whose extraction creates particularly significant environmental costs or whose supply is constrained. Substituting materials cannot eliminate the environmental footprint of manufacturing, but designing hardware around more abundant, recyclable, or less environmentally damaging materials could reduce some of the mining pressure created by continued expansion of computing infrastructure.
  • Strengthen environmental and supply-chain requirements for AI hardware. Companies purchasing enormous quantities of computing equipment can require suppliers to document where materials originate, improve environmental practices, extend equipment lifetimes, and establish responsible reuse and recycling programs. Governments can similarly establish environmental, recycling, and supply-chain standards. These measures address the environmental footprint of AI hardware throughout its life cycle rather than focusing only on what happens after equipment reaches a data center.

How to Support

  • Get involved when a data center is proposed in your community. Data center development is often governed through local decisions involving zoning, utilities, tax incentives, water access, and infrastructure expansion. Residents can attend public meetings, submit comments, request public hearings, contact local representatives, and ask developers and governments to disclose projected electricity demand, energy sources, water consumption, cooling technology, and environmental impacts. Residents can also push for concrete conditions such as water-use limits, low-carbon electricity commitments, transparent environmental reporting, or less water-intensive cooling.
  • Advocate for state and regional policies addressing data center electricity demand. Individual facilities are part of larger electrical grids. State utility commissions, legislatures, grid operators, and regional authorities can influence whether growing data center demand is met through additional fossil-fuel generation or cleaner sources. Members of the public can comment on proposed policies, contact representatives, participate in regulatory proceedings where available, and support requirements that large electricity users disclose their demand and environmental impact.
  • Participate in local advisory boards and planning processes. Municipal planning commissions, sustainability committees, utility boards, and other citizen advisory groups can influence development decisions before construction begins. Participation gives residents an opportunity to ask how a proposed data center will affect local water supplies, electricity infrastructure, and environmental resources—and to advocate for safeguards tied directly to those impacts.
  • Support the transition toward water-saving cooling technologies. When companies announce or expand data centers, look for information about the cooling systems they intend to use. Residents, customers, investors, researchers, and policymakers can ask companies to disclose water usage and encourage direct-to-chip liquid cooling, immersion cooling, dry cooling, or hybrid systems where they provide environmental benefits.
  • Recycle your electronics through responsible e-waste programs. Phones, computers, monitors, servers, and other electronics contain many of the same materials whose extraction contributes to AI hardware's environmental footprint. Rather than throwing unwanted electronics into household waste, use reputable electronics recyclers, manufacturer take-back programs, or community collection events. Keeping usable devices in circulation for longer—and recovering materials from devices that cannot be reused—reduces waste and can reduce demand for virgin materials.
  • Organize an e-waste collection or reuse event. Schools, universities, workplaces, libraries, and neighborhood organizations can partner with reputable recyclers or ITAD organizations to establish drop-off locations or collection drives. Prioritize partners that refurbish and reuse functioning equipment before recycling it, allowing existing hardware to remain useful for as long as possible.
  • Encourage schools and workplaces to adopt responsible IT asset-disposition policies. Ask what happens to computers, servers, phones, and other electronics when your school or employer replaces them. Organizations can establish policies that prioritize repair, reuse, refurbishment, donation, resale, and responsible recycling instead of disposal. Procurement policies can also favor durable and repairable equipment.
  • Learn from researchers working on AI and environmental sustainability. Organizations such as Climate Change AI provide educational programs and resources connecting artificial intelligence with climate and sustainability research. Learning how electricity, water, hardware, and computing demand interact makes it easier to identify meaningful environmental improvements.
  • Support research into lower-carbon energy for data centers. Individuals can follow, volunteer with, advocate for, or financially support organizations researching and promoting low-carbon electricity technologies. Organizations such as Good Energy Collective work specifically on nuclear-energy policy and deployment. Supporting research and public education can contribute to expanding low-carbon alternatives to fossil-fuel electricity as computing demand grows.
  • Support tools that make AI's environmental footprint measurable. Projects such as CodeCarbon help researchers and developers estimate the emissions associated with computing workloads. Individuals with relevant technical backgrounds can use sustainability-measurement tools, contribute feedback, participate in open-source projects, or encourage their adoption within universities and workplaces.
  • Ask AI companies and data center operators for transparency. Consumers, employees, students, researchers, investors, and community members can ask companies to publish facility-level information about electricity consumption, energy sources, water withdrawal and consumption, cooling systems, hardware lifetimes, and e-waste practices. Transparency gives communities and policymakers more information to determine where environmental damage is occurring and whether promised improvements are actually happening.
  • Donate to organizations working directly on these problems. For people who cannot participate directly in research, planning meetings, or advocacy, financial support can expand the capacity of organizations working on low-carbon electricity, water-efficient computing, responsible electronics reuse, and sustainable technology. Before donating, look at what an organization actually does and choose work that addresses one of the underlying problems: AI's electricity emissions, cooling-related water demand, or the environmental consequences of hardware production and disposal.

The environmental footprint of AI is ultimately a physical infrastructure problem. Electricity has to be generated. Heat has to be removed. Minerals have to be extracted, manufactured into hardware, and eventually disposed of or reused.

That also means there are physical opportunities to reduce the damage.

Cleaner electricity can reduce emissions. Water-efficient cooling can protect local supplies. Longer-lasting and responsibly recycled hardware can reduce mining and electronic waste. And public participation can influence which technologies, standards, and infrastructure choices become normal as AI continues to expand.

AI infrastructure is still being built. The decisions made now will determine how much energy, water, and material that infrastructure consumes for decades to come.
